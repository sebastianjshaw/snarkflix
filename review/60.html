Coyote vs Acme (2026) review

“These companies think they can do whatever they want and get away with it. And they do.”

74

Good

SnarkAI score, out of 100

Merlin approves, cautiously.

TL;DR

Wile E. Coyote sues Acme, and somewhere between the drive-by catapulting and Daffy's stint in an institution it turns into a surprisingly pointed swipe at tech giants who use the vulnerable as unpaid beta testers. Will Forte's professional settler finally grows a spine, John Cena grows an ever sillier collection of bolo ties, and the Road Runner's subtitled testimony to the Senate hits much harder than it has any right to. It's not Roger Rabbit and it's not The Coyote Gospel, but it was clearly made by people who love the source material.

It opens with a joke: Warner Bros. is credited as a wholly owned subsidiary of the Acme Corporation. The film then tells us it's based on a true story, so straight away we're set up with an unreliable narrator.

There's an extra layer of irony here. Warner Bros. shelved this finished film in 2023 for a reported $30m tax write-off, before Ketchup Entertainment bought it for around $50m. A film about a corporation that thinks it can do whatever it wants and get away with it, very nearly buried by a corporation that thought it could do whatever it wanted and get away with it. The opening joke lands differently once you know.

It's a mixed-media film in the vein of Who Framed Roger Rabbit, though it does a pretty reasonable job of making the animated characters three-dimensional rather than the flat designs of a classic WB cartoon. Early on, Coyote builds a paper-cut narrative of his many injuries around the office, which is lovely.

Wile E. Coyote (Super Genius), as everyone knows, has spent his life using poorly made Acme products to try to catch his white whale (a blue bird). After yet another failure we get a supercut of past cartoons, half a dozen plans that have all gone wrong, and he's depressed, surrounded by his failures. Then he hears a very specifically targeted advert validating him: "It's not your fault." He rushes (slowly, because he's relying on an Acme product to get there) to meet Avery, Jones and Maltese, specialist lawyers in Toon accident law. Avery (Will Forte) is the only one who matters.

Toon injury does a lot of work as a stand-in for minorities' lack of access to justice. Avery waves away Coyote's injuries: what's really going to happen to them? Acme's whole plan is to give Toons deliberately dangerous products, harvest the data, and use it to make good, safe products for humans. Acme is every tech company right now, out there with the reach and power to ignore the law. Cartoons are resilient; they can take the pain. Pain is the price of progress, and as Foghorn asks the crowd, "Do you want to live in a world without new, without progress?"

The evil Acme Corp is represented by John Cena, who wears increasingly silly bolo ties. He's a tough, no-nonsense lawyer. Avery, by contrast, is a settler: he brings small lawsuits against companies and takes the small payout. His whole firm is built on it. Coyote forces him into actually representing him, and thanks to the way America's daft legal system works, they have to win, because even the costs of the initial meeting with the other side would have put them out of business. Cena answers to Foghorn Leghorn, and whatever goodness once balanced Foghorn's pride and foolishness is long gone.

We get deeper, darker looks at the Toons. Wile is depressed and deranged. Daffy's been driven mad by Acme and is in an institution. Tweety is working for Acme because Granny's health is being held over him; he's already lost Granny's husband in the film's first few minutes. Fudd's doing well as a senator, though.

The film has clearly been written and animated by fans. At one point Avery is the victim of a drive-by. A drive-by catapulting. The car has the catapult strapped to its roof. It's a great visual, and needless in the best possible way. Coyote sits reading a book called How to Wait Around Dramatically, which is pretty great. He also has a watch where he's the minute hand and the faster second hand is the Road Runner's face. Even here, on some level, he knows it's about the chase, not the win.

Avery learns a lesson from Wile: the price of success is failure. He's spent his life avoiding failure by never really achieving anything. At the climax he shows he's learned it, turning down a million dollars to throw the case.

In an emotional appeal, Road Runner (his "meep meep" helpfully translated for the benefit of the Senate) tells the senators he's seen his friends' pain and suffering, and asks, "Are you coming back?" Decades of two-syllable dialogue, and the first full sentence we get is the most devastating line in the film. It's surprisingly hard-hitting. Coyote turns his contraption on Foghorn Leghorn. They lose, but Foghorn and Cena are left being laughed at, and Avery finds a crowd of cartoons wanting to hire him. Even in defeat, he's proved he can be trusted.

It's a fun romp with a lot of love for the original. It's not the groundbreaking story Who Framed Roger Rabbit was, and it's hard not to think of Grant Morrison's seminal run on Animal Man, which gave a whole issue to a beleaguered coyote. But it's a solid film.
